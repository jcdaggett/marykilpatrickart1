You Can Sell Them When I Croak

For more than thirty years, Mary Kilpatrick’s paintings filled the walls and halls of her homes, from New York City to Bend, Oregon. Her husband, Howard Schor, asked her again and again to show them, to let him find a gallery, to sell them beyond family and the occasional art-loving friend. Her answer stayed the same: “No. I love to look at them. You can sell them when I croak.”

“No. I love to look at them. You can sell them when I croak.”

Then, about a year ago, out of the blue — which is how many of Mary’s decisions arrive — she changed her mind. “OK, you can sell them, but I don’t want anything to do with it. I will just do what I always do.”

“What’s that?” Howard asked.

“I just do it.”

This is an introduction to what all that doing produced: more than 130 paintings and well over 100 sketches made between 1988 and 2026. Mary is a self-taught artist who spent her first career on the stage, from Broadway to national tours, and nine years as a Children’s Specialist five blocks from Ground Zero. She moved to Bend, Oregon, in 2002, and in 2006 she co-founded the very successful 501(c)(3) nonprofit BEAT (Bend Experimental Art Theatre). She retired in 2016 as artistic director and became a full-time artist. BEAT celebrates its twentieth year in 2026. Her art is a world of women — every one of them painted with the same bold, direct, generous hand. Mary has a name for the outlook behind them: Ultra Feminism.

What Ultra Feminism Means

Ultra Feminism is Mary’s own phrase, and it is as generous as she is. It embraces women in all their variety, including grandmothers, strippers, churchgoers, homemakers and the occasional male. Mary’s paintings are alive with mythic archetypes: the Mother, the Muse, the Saint, the Siren, the Rebel, the Survivor. All psyches are free to be. A woman sets the terms of her own identity, autonomy, sexuality and power, and every one of her ways of being belongs in the picture.

Mary loves and celebrates men. “I adore men,” she observes, “and they are generally less complex than women.” She holds men in deep respect — their intelligence, their compassion, their love. “They are just not as interesting for me to paint.” Women are another realm. Every aspect of them, their personalities and their physicality, fascinates and intrigues her.

Ultra Feminism is an ideal to reach for, a step beyond everyday life. Its moral code fits in a single line. Asked for one, Mary offers: “It’s ok unless it’s evil.” Whatever a woman chooses for herself is as good as she makes it. What is forced on her is the evil confronted in her darkest paintings. Everything else is life itself. For Mary personally, all heartfelt paths lead to God.

“It’s ok unless it’s evil.”

Seven Convictions, Seven Ways In

01

A woman authors herself

Mary spent her life in the theater, and she knows the difference between being made into a spectacle and choosing to become one. Her women choose. In Showgirl, the dancer knows every eye is on her and enjoys it — amused, self-possessed and at home in the act of being seen, with years of discipline and hard work behind the glamour. Masked carries Mary’s own punchline: “Surprise, I’m a showgirl.” Josephine Baker, painted from three photographs, dances as three figures at once, standing in for everyone who celebrates their heritage. She decides how she is seen. In Bottoms Up, “whether she chooses to lead, to follow, to ride, or to be ridden,” she’s doing it because she loves it. Plug takes us back to a beginning: Gypsy Rose Lee as a gawky kid, a chrysalis before the performer emerges, under Mary’s note, “Talent can bloom.”

02

The costume is part of the person

Mary once played several roles in a single production of Heidi, and her paintings hold the same truth: a woman carries many selves at once, and every one of them is real. Personas builds a woman from squares of fabric, roses and ornament — a living collage. Virgin folds tenderness, sexuality, strength and vulnerability into one many-colored body. A virgin idea? What if Cubism were soft, like nature and the feminine? “Aren’t we unpredictable and filled with color? Picasso gave us Cubism, this is me doing ‘Ovalism,’” she teases. Two Dancers shares the style: its circular forms and trailing lines add a mischievous, almost surreal quality, and the visual joke is mirrored in the dancers’ impossible positions. “Curved beauties. More Ovalism,” Mary laughs. Rising Spirit carries her advice for the hard days: “Taking your mask off helps toward loving yourself.”

03

Joy and injury, painted with one hand

Party Time, Flying High and Rape share the same bright, flat, direct visual language. Mary paints a ski run and a sexual assault with the same hand because both belong to the same life. She paints her truth as she feels it in the moment; the painting quite literally paints itself. The result is startling range. The same painter made Rape and Resting, in which her niece escapes a crowded family party for a few quiet minutes on the pot.

04

Sensuality, dignity and power belong together

Earth Mother is an eruption of feminine energy. “I so respect the evolving of diverse cultures — Blacks, Native Americans, Asians, Jews and Women of all ages and types, for putting up with [many] White men.” Strong women, in her eyes, are grounded, sensual and true forces of nature: “They should be boldly displayed and emulated.” Tickle comes from New York at the turn of the century, when “I was stripping between acting gigs”; Mary adds “a tendrilled reed to introduce mirth and motion into a sexual experience that is often so serious and mechanical.” Lovers is “love in the abstract,” where “there is no state of mind, only a state of being.” And Hippie Girl is Mary in the Haight, “just relaxing during my hippie days.”

05

Freedom lives inside belonging

Mary’s free spirit has room for faith. In Freedom, “these Amish girls are out for a walk and the brave one picks the apple.” Courage looks like curiosity, and the other girls stay right beside her. Christ Mass finds holiness in a spindly tree with three bulbs: devotion shaped by modest means and free of commercialism. Breaking Away says it plainly: “We must be free to accept or reject the traditions we are born into.” Mary’s own faith is just as open. Asked whether she believes in God: “Of course all paths lead to God.” And evil? “That path tried to get to God but is a dead end.”

06

Bearing witness

In 2001 the September 11 attacks struck five blocks from Sanctuary for Families, the domestic-violence shelter where Mary worked as a Children’s Specialist. She erupted, and Rape, Protectors, Bravery and Unclean poured out. Rape is “my expression of outrage at a culture that has too often been rooted in patriarchy”; she wanted “an image that confronts the viewer rather than comforts them.” Protectors shows Muslim women guarding one another in a newly hostile America. Unclean began with a conversation. Mary recalls: “After 9/11, a Muslim co-worker at Sanctuary for Families told me that in her faith, a woman is unclean during menstruation. I threw up my arms — Whaaat!” Out flowed a big woman, crouched and bare, isolated in a vast, small box of blue. That Christmas, Mary and Howard completed their acclaimed children’s book, A Place for Starr. Innovatively told from a child’s point of view, with eighteen original illustrations and Howard’s poem, it chronicles a family’s journey out of a life of domestic violence. It has sold more than 15,000 copies, and a third edition is available through a partnership with Amazon. Soon after, Mary gave seven months’ notice, met her childhood friend Nancy in San Francisco and headed north to make a new home in Bend, Oregon.

Other paintings also bear witness. Abuse came after she was booed off a strip-club stage as a “skinny old woman.” Kidnapped I and II tell the true story of actress Choi Eun-hee and her husband, director Shin Sang-ok, abducted by North Korean agents in 1978. Slavery: “children picking cotton speaks for all those enslaved.” Trapped is a real dog Mary saw: “Animal abuse is equivalent to baby abuse.” War, in black and white, is “a depiction, sans religion – of hell.” Each painting asks what remains a woman’s own when power is turned against her. Mary’s answer is presence, identity and dignity. Her women remain fully clothed in it.

07

Together

When Mary paints women in groups, they move as one. In Harmony, women in a progression of blues sing to an unseen audience. In Togetherness, women of every color sit in one unbroken line: “We are so close in our differences… To dream is not naive.” Party Time turns The Vagina Monologues, in which Mary performed, into a sky of balloons: “every woman’s story, most of them flying high.”

How She Paints

“I favor symbolism over realism and abstraction,” Mary says, then adds quizzically, “And yet I often choose to contain my figures in sharp lined form.” Her figures are held in a strong black line, pushed into bold, stylized shapes and lit with big, expressive color. You will hardly find a straight line in her work, and if you do, it is invariably on an angle.

How a woman is turned in a Mary Kilpatrick painting is part of what she’s saying. When the meaning is indirect, she turns her back, looks over her shoulder or stands in profile. When it is direct, she faces you full front, with a present, living face.

Then there are the materials: feathers, pearls, gems and fabric worked right into the surface. The stuff of sewing baskets and costume trunks, long labeled “women’s craft,” is not just ornamentation; there is a reason for it, especially in a painting like Personas. In Mary’s hand, ornament is substance. Nature runs through everything — flowers, reeds, trees, water — because for Mary, nature and the feminine are one.

Ask her about method and she waves a hand. “I’m a gypsy. I don’t overintellectualize anything. I just get on my mark and go.” She dreams her feelings into life.

In Her Own Words

Mary, with true innocence, often expresses her worldview in sage, sideways turns of phrase — malaprops with a wisdom all their own. Her women are always “dressed to the tens,” and when it comes to her art, “the truth is in the pudding.” Her humor runs straight into the paintings. Cotton Candy began with Dolly Parton and Marie Antoinette: “Both symbolize excessive fun — of course, the head in the box…” Cat Lady arrived while Mary was ruing the loss of Kamala Harris, and Hunter, the family cat, came flying in: “She leaped up and poof, I thought, what a metaphor for going nowhere.” And of her mother, painted in Geri: “My mother was a simple woman who was majestic.”

At seventy-six, Mary is the best dancer in her twice-weekly Zumba and hip-hop class of sixty women and three men. She is proud to have learned to text, and she loves scrolling the cat video reels on Facebook — what is beautiful to Mary is beautiful. “I don’t know anything about AI, but I absolutely love the art of Kelly Boesch.” Ask what she plans to do next, and the list is ready: “Paint, act, love you [Howard] and our cats, ride my bike and hope for a good ski season.”

Why Now

Art by women about women’s lives is having its moment. Faith Ringgold’s painted story quilts set a $1.57 million auction record in 2024, following major museum retrospectives. Joan Semmel, who paints the female body from a woman’s point of view, won wide recognition late in life. Carmen Herrera sold her first painting at eighty-nine.

Mary Kilpatrick belongs in that story: a self-taught fine artist with stylized figures, sharp humor and a frank love of the female body, in the line of Gladys Nilsson and the Chicago Imagists. Her catalog is fresh to the market — fifty-six works offered now, with many more, including a sketch series, not presently being offered to the public. Many have been seen only by friends and family, with more than a dozen works already in private collections from New York to Arizona. And the work keeps its edge: Virtual Girl, painted in 2024, speaks directly to today’s debate over artificial intelligence.

A Word from Howard

To understand Mary’s work, you need to know her, or at least know about her. I have been with her for thirty-eight years, married since 1998. Trite but true: we are still as in love as we have ever been.

Everyone who meets Mary — in her dance class, in a cast, at a party or at the market — comes away with the same impression: a unique, loquacious and empathetic tour de force. She is one of the most direct, guileless, compassionate, dynamic and simple women you will ever meet. Her philosophy of painting is a direct mirror of her philosophy of life.

Her instructions to me: “Go ahead, make me famous — but if you croak, I am giving my paintings away as I please.”

“Go ahead, make me famous — but if you croak, I am giving my paintings away as I please.”
